A wet floor beside the water heater is a call we hear about constantly, and the first question is always the same: where is it actually coming from? Water travels along pipes and collects wherever the floor dips, so the puddle is often nowhere near the source. It might be the temperature and pressure relief valve or its discharge line, the drain valve near the bottom of the tank, the inlet and outlet fittings on top, condensation on a cold tank, or a nearby pipe that has nothing to do with the heater. It might also be the tank itself, and that answer changes everything. In Carmichael, CA, we start every leak call by finding the true source.

Finding the source is a process, not a glance. We clear and dry the area, then watch where new water appears. The relief valve and its discharge pipe get checked first, followed by the drain valve and the inlet and outlet fittings, where dielectric unions and flex connectors tend to corrode. If the heater is gas-fired, we look inside the burner compartment, since water there can be condensation or a sign the tank has given out. When the relief valve is involved, we measure water pressure and check whether expansion has anywhere to go. We recommend nothing until the source is confirmed.

The repair depends on what we found. Valve and fitting leaks are the good news: relief valves, drain valves, dielectric nipples, and flex connectors ride on our trucks, and we can often add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve the same day if pressure caused the problem. A leak from the tank body is the bad news, because a rusted-through tank is past saving, and we will say so rather than sell a patch. In both cases, we help limit future damage with a properly drained pan, a leak alarm or automatic shutoff, and a quick lesson on your shutoff valve. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Corroded pipe connections or damp flex lines above the heater
  • The water meter moving when nothing in the house is running
  • A weeping drain spigot on the lower front of the heater
  • Water inside the burner compartment of a gas heater
  • Water collecting under or beside the heater
  • Rusty or discolored hot water paired with dampness at the base
  • Moisture or mineral crust at the end of the relief valve pipe
  • Warped baseboards or stained drywall beside the water heater

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

A leak looks like a simple job until you try to find it. Water can start at a top fitting, run down under the jacket, and show up at the base, and condensation on cold tanks add confusion. A wrong part is not the only risk. Plugging a dripping relief valve removes the protection against overheating and overpressure, forcing a corroded connection can crack it wide open, and electric heaters bring high-voltage wiring into a wet area. When the tank itself is the source, no DIY fix will hold for long.

With a trained eye, the answer usually comes quickly. We rule out each possible source one by one, confirm it with fresh water on a dried surface, and measure static pressure so a new valve does not start dripping next week. Worn relief valves, weeping drain valves, and corroded connections are replaced on the spot, and pressure problems get a real solution, whether that is an expansion tank or a pressure-reducing valve. A rusted-through tank gets an honest replacement recommendation instead of a temporary fix, and we can add a piped drain pan and a leak sensor to protect your home going forward.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Here's where to begin:

  • Put a water leak sensor on the floor next to the tank so a drip gets noticed early
  • Make a habit of checking around the heater for moisture or corrosion every few weeks
  • Install a drain pan piped to a safe spot, which matters most for attic, closet, and upstairs heaters
  • Make sure a closed plumbing system has a working expansion tank to absorb heated water
  • Annual flushing removes sediment that traps heat against the tank floor
  • Have household pressure measured, since high pressure wears out relief valves and fittings faster
  • Inspecting the anode rod periodically is one of the best ways to delay a tank leak
